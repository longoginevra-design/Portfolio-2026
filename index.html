01 — How I work

  1. 01

    Learn the work

    Before the screen. How the job gets done, by whom, under what conditions — gloves and bad light, a safety procedure, a seller with four minutes. In a technical domain the user knows more than I do on day one.

  2. 02

    Find where the value is

    The brief is rarely the problem. I look for the thing that would actually move — a build cost, a sales cycle, a part ruined by one wrong parameter — and design toward that instead of toward the request.

  3. 03

    Build the architecture

    One foundation, many outputs. Variables, tokens, patterns, a grid someone else can extend without me. This is the phase that decides whether the second product costs as much as the first.

  4. 04

    Ship it, then keep it alive

    Next to front-end through the build, refining whatever is costing the team effort. Then adoption — documentation at the level people actually work at, and being there when someone hits a wall.

Twice in the last two years, phase 02 produced work nobody had asked for — and it became the most valuable thing we delivered.

03 — Where I'm going

Consulting taught me to get useful fast in domains I didn't know. What it doesn't give you is the second year on the same product.

Machinery, manufacturing, public services, banking — every project meant learning a field from scratch and being useful in it within weeks. That's the skill I'd bring anywhere. But the part I'm missing is the one where you stay: where you find out which of your decisions actually held, and you're the one who has to live with the ones that didn't.

I'm looking for a product team in a technical or scientific domain — instrumentation, industrial software, engineering and developer tools, anything where the user knows more about their work than I do on day one and the interface has to earn its place. Complex, data-dense, close to engineering. That's the environment where I do my best work, and I'd rather go deeper into one of those than wider across five more.

Case 01 — Designing to ship

Two machines, two brands,
one HMI foundation.

Designed, systematised, and taken into development alongside the client's engineering team. Eight configurations from a single foundation, deadlines met, and a client who adopted our tokens in their own codebase.

Product decisions under physical constraints Shipping alongside engineering Systems that scale
Role
Design LeadBoth products, design system architecture, implementation oversight
Client
Industrial OEM group, ~€390M, built through European acquisitions
Period
January — December 2025
Team
3 designers, plus the client's front-end and back-end teams
Surface
Embedded HMI · two screen sizes · light and dark mode · physical controls
Environment
Warehouse floor — gloves, noise, changing light, safety procedures

Context

The product is an aluminium profile cutting machine. Operators set it up before and during production — with gloves on, in a noisy warehouse, under bad and changing light, inside safety procedures where a wrong parameter isn't a UI error but a ruined part.

Every one of those conditions became a constraint: larger touch targets for gloved hands, no reliance on audio, redundant visual states, contrast verified past the WCAG 2.1 AA floor, explicit confirmation on anything that changes the cut.

The problem

The group had grown by acquisition, absorbing European companies with their own conventions. The strategy was coherence without flattening: distinct brands, one way of interacting with the machine.

The cost of fragmentation was already visible — every product started over. The breaking point was concrete: two products, two screen resolutions, tight dev deadlines, and a starting plan to design and maintain each combination one by one.

Decisions

  • →

    Shared navigation, brand at the visual layer

    Same structure across both products; brand lives in the visuals. The reason is usage before efficiency — an operator who learns one machine can run another in the group, even in a different tier, because the language is the same. That's one brand built from experience, not from a logo.

    It cost the brands the ability to differentiate structurally, so I defended it by showing the work: screens side by side, and what changed for the person using them.

  • →

    Task-oriented over dense

    Product owners wanted to minimise clicks, and their conclusion was: show everything. I kept the goal and changed the route. With gloves and bad light, hunting for one parameter in a wall of numbers doesn't save time.

    Screens show only what the current task needs — on the execution page, selected profile parameters appear up front only if they're mandatory and always useful; secondary ones sit one level away, in tabs. On the smaller display the same principle got stricter rather than dropped: limited space made mandatory what had been a choice.
